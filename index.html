Our Story / Historical Review

From the very early days of my training as a urologist at the Hammersmith Hospital, the Royal Postgraduate Medical School, the Institute of Urology and St. Peter’s Hospitals in London, England, my teachers, my colleagues and I became aware of the cost, morbidity and mortality associated with the use of the Foley balloon Catheter. My urology training was later refined in the US at the University of South Florida and at Bay Pines VA Medical Center in Florida as a Professor of Urology.

Working as a scientist, inventor and urology educator at the University of South Florida for over 25 years, the inventor came to the conclusion as early as 1995 that the current three different urinary catheters in the medical field need to be changed. The reason is the morbidity and even mortality of their use, in addition to the staggering cost to treat CAUTI (Catheter Associated Urinary Tract Infection).

— Said Ismail Hakki, M.D., Ph.D., Inventor

The balloon has had its day

The basic Foley design has barely moved since 1933. Its inflatable-balloon anchor, tied to urethral trauma, retained urine and patient discomfort, went largely unquestioned for decades. That changes with this catheter.

Lotus offers a genuinely different way to hold a catheter in place: self-opening wings take the position of the inflatable balloon in an indwelling device made for current urological practice. It is cleared through FDA 510(k) (K191512) and intended to improve patient outcomes while lowering the overall cost of care.

One device, several roles

What it stands in for

The Lotus No-Balloon Catheter takes over the work of three older devices, all without a balloon.

Nights of uninterrupted sleep

Takes the place of a straight catheter

People using intermittent straight catheters often wake several times a night to catheterize again, which wrecks sleep and quality of life. Lotus is FDA-cleared for intermittent catheterization and works through a self-contained bellows mechanism, with no balloon or syringe, so patients can use it at home as an indwelling catheter.

Staying in place without harm

Takes the place of a Foley catheter

A Foley balloon bears on the trigone and bladder neck while its tip bears on the bladder dome, leading to granuloma, retained urine, bladder spasm and the conditions behind catheter-associated infections (CAUTI). Lotus drops the balloon completely and anchors with soft, deployable wings that rest gently, without pressing on or irritating the trigone and bladder neck. In the clinical study, drainage was nearly complete, with a mean post-void residual of only 4.8 mL.

Simpler from the outset

Takes the place of a Malecot catheter

The Malecot needs a stiff metal or plastic introducer to change the 3-4 wing or flower configuration to a straight catheter to pass through a created hole surgically. Lotus carries FDA clearance for the same indications, and its self-contained bellows mechanism removes any need for stylets.

Comparison between the Foley balloon catheter and the Lotus catheter

I- Foley Balloon Catheter

FDA Approved Indication for Use:

A- Urinary bladder drainage and or irrigation through the urethra.

1- The Foley Catheter is stabilized inside the urinary bladder using an inflatable balloon filled with 5cc-30cc sterile water. The water is non-compressible. The diameter of this non compressible balloon ranges between 2.5-centimeters- 4.5 centimeters (one inch-1.77 inch).

2- This balloon inflated with sterile water weighs approximately 5.0 grams -30 grams sits on the most sensitive part of the urinary bladder i.e. trigone and the bladder neck. This balloon is the sole stabilizer that keeps the Foley catheter indwelling inside the urinary bladder. The external stabilizer used to adhere the Foley catheter to the inner side of the thigh using special stabilizer or cross strong adhesive tape is to prevent continued friction of the inflated balloon against the most sensitive part of the bladder. This stabilizer is also used to prevent a confused or demented patient forcibly removing the Foley catheter with the balloon fully inflated. Thus causing immediate and lasting damage.

3- The Foley catheter’s two drainage ports are located at the tip of the Foley catheter above the balloon. This tip is about 2.5-centimeter -3.5 centimeter long. Due to the high location of the drainage port. There is residual urine ranging between 10-100cc inside the urinary bladder at all times, Roger C.L. Feneley et al.

It is well known that the first sensation to urinate starts when there is 50 cc. of urine in the bladder. Therefore, the presence of an inflated balloon (5.0 cc-30 cc), plus residual urine averaging 30 cc- 50 cc. Most patients sense the urge to urinate as long as the Foley catheter is indwelling causing painful bladder spasm that necessitates the administration of antispasmodic medication, and at time analgesic medication.

4- The tip of the Foley catheter housing the 2 drainage ports is usually 2.5 centimeters-4.5 centimeters long. This tip rests on the balloon which is filled with non-compressible fluid (water). This tip pushes against the dome of the bladder upon which all bowels rest. Thus, within 24-48 hours 100 % of all patients with an indwelling Foley catheter develops catheter granuloma. Microscopically all catheter granulomas contain acute as well as chronic inflammatory cells.

In summary, the combination of high residual urine, with the balloon of the Foley catheter constantly irritating the trigone and bladder neck, with tip of the Foley catheter pushing against the dome developing microscopic evidence of acute/chronic inflammatory cells (cystitis) as seen endoscopically in every single case with an indwelling Foley catheter. Thus, there are many factors in the Foley balloon catheter that causes infection, CAUTI (Catheter Associated Urinary Tract Infection) in patients. This CAUTI results in staggering cost, morbidity and even mortality, Roger C. L. Feneley et al.

5- The force needed to dislodge the Foley catheter with the balloon fully inflated ranges between 5 Pounds- 50 Pounds (2.268 kilogram- 20.26 kilogram force). This is not an uncommon incidence. This may cause severe bleeding and tear of the bladder neck and urethra with immediate and late complications.

II- Lotus No Balloon Catheter

FDA Approved Indication For Use:

A- Urinary bladder drainage and irrigation as in a Foley Catheter.

B- Suprapubic Cystostomy and Nephrostomy drainage and irrigation as in a Malecot Catheter.

C- Clean Intermittent Catheterization as in a Straight Catheter.

1- The 2-3 wing flower configuration of the Lotus Catheter is made of rubber polymer (Silicone, TPU, PVC, and Latex).

This rubber polymer is compressible and collapsible. In addition, the tip of the Lotus Catheter is only 0.5 centimeters in length and sits on the 2-3 wing compressible collapsible rubber. Furthermore, there is no residual urine in patients with the Lotus Catheter indwelling. Most importantly, the 2-3 wing configuration does not sit on the most sensitive part of the bladder ie trigone and bladder neck.

2- The 2-3 wing flower configuration is not the primary stabilizer keeping the Lotus Catheter indwelling. It is a backup. The primary stability for the Lotus Catheter is firm adherence to the medial side of the patient’s thigh using special Lotus Stabilizer or cross firm adhesive tape.

3- The force needed to dislodge the fully deployed 2-3 wing flower configuration is 2-3 Pound (0.90 kilogram- 1.36 kilogram). The reason is the collapsible rubber polymer of the 2-3 wing flower configuration. Minimal or no injury occurs in accidental pull of the Lotus Catheter with the 2-3 wings fully deployed.

Urinary Catheters

There are Four Urinary Catheters in the field of medicine worldwide. Each catheter has an FDA distinctive and a specific instructions for use.

1- The Foley Balloon Catheter

FDA Indications for Use: Continuous Urinary Bladder Drainage and Or Irrigation Through the urethra.

The Foley Catheter has been with us for about 100 years now. It is a catheter with a balloon to be inflated using sterile water via a side channel. The average inflation of the balloon is 10-30 cc of sterile water. The average inflated Foley balloon thus weighs 10-30 grams.

The balloon acts as stabilizing force to keep the Foley Catheter indwelling inside the urinary bladder for continuous drainage and or irrigation of the bladder. This 10-30 grams of non distensible fluid sit on the most sensitive part of the urinary bladder. The trigone and bladder neck causing continuous painful spasm as long as the catheter is indwelling, Continuous medication is usually necessary to alleviate the pain and discomfort the patient usually experiences. Accidental pull out of the Foley Catheter with the balloon fully inflated is not rare. The force needed to pull through the Foley with the balloon fully inflated is anywhere between, 10-50 Pounds force. Such force may partially or completely destroy the internal sphincter mechanism of the urinary bladder leading to partial or complete incontinence. It is for all such reasons the Foley Catheter Standard of Care is to stabilize the Foley Catheter by firmly attaching the Foley Catheter to the inner side of the patient’s thigh using adhesive tape, Velcro or an adhesive stabilizer specific to the Foley Catheter.

The presence of the drainage holes above the balloon usually lead to the presence of 10c-100cc of residual urine, Roger C.L. Feneley. Residual urine is the main cause of CAUTI (Catheter Associated Urinary Tract Infection). Not only that, such residual urine with the weight of the fully inflated balloon would give the patients a continuous sense of urgency to urinate. Uncontrolled urine may leak around the Foley Catheter soiling the patient, his clothes and his surroundings.

Conclusion:

The balloon of the Foley Catheter filled at least with 10cc-30cc of non-distensible sterile water acts as the stabilizer preventing the Foley Catheter from slippage while indwelling inside the bladder. The balloon uses the most sensitive and important part of the urinary bladder as a stabilizer. Namely, the Trigone and bladder neck as the stabilizer. The standard of care to stabilize the Foley Catheter by using an adhesive tape or special adhesive stabilizer specific for the Foley Catheter fixed to the inner side of the patient’s thigh is merely protective. This is to lessen or ease the continuous irritation by the Foley balloon to the Trigone and bladder neck. Also to protect the Foley Catheter with the balloon fully inflated from being accidentally or intentionally pulled out by the patient with the balloon fully inflated. A force of 10-50 Pounds is needed to dislodge the Foley balloon from the bladder neck and urethra. Thus, causing tears of the bladder neck and urethra that might have serious immediate and long-term complications.

2- The Malecot Catheter

The Malecot Catheter has been with us for over 120 years now. It is used almost exclusively by urologists. The Malecot needs a stiff metal or plastic introducer to change the 3-4 wing or flower configuration to a straight catheter to pass through a created hole surgically. This created hole could be in the lower abdomen to drain the urinary bladder as in suprapubic cystostomy. Alternatively, this surgically created hole is in the flank of the patient to drain urine from the renal pelvis as in Nephrostomy.

Once the flower configuration is located by direct vision or via fluoroscopy to be inside the urinary bladder as in suprapubic Cystostomy. Once the flower configuration is inside the renal pelvis as located by direct vision or fluoroscopy. Then the stiff metal or plastic introducer is withdrawn. The straight Malecot catheter would revert back to 3-4 wing flower configuration. In order to remove the Malecot Catheter the stiff metal or plastic introducer is passed through the lumen of the Malecot. Again introducing the stiff metal or plastic introducer is passed to the inside of the tip of the Malecot Catheter under direct vision or through fluoroscopy. Then by pulling on the shaft the Malecot towards the operator. The 3-4 wing flower configuration would change into a straight catheter again. The operator can pull out the straight Malecot catheter from the urinary bladder suprapubically as in suprapubic cystostomy or from the renal pelvis as in Nephrostomy.

Indications for Use:

A- Suprapubic Cystostomy. Through a surgically created hole in the lower abdomen just above the pubic bone. The urinary bladder is drained for a long period of time and sometimes for the life of the patient.

B- Nephrostomy. Through a surgically created hole in the flank of the patient connected to the pelvis of the kidney. The Malecot Catheter would drain the urine from the renal pelvis. This could be for days, or weeks or for the life of the patient. In the event of the presence of a JJ ureteral stent. Specialty Lotus Catheters should be used. This is to avoid inadvertent pull out of the JJ by one of the standard 2-3 wings of the Lotus Catheter.

3- The Straight Catheter

The straight catheter has been with us since early civilization. The Egyptians were first to describe draining the urinary bladder using a stiff hollow bone or a leather tube lubricated using butter or cheese. Then we see ancient Indian, ancient Chinese description.

It was not until 180 years or so when synthetic polymers were used. The straight catheter is a straight hollow malleable tube lubricated and passed urethrally to empty the bladder. Since the advent of the Malecot and Foley balloon Catheter. The straight catheter use is restricted by the FDA to intermittent self catheterization in patient who are unable to empty their bladder. Failure to empty a distended urinary bladder would lead to back pressure to both kidneys. This would end in renal failure and death.

Indications for Use:

The Straight Catheter is used for Clean Self Intermittent Catheterization by the patient. The Straight Catheters catheterization kit usually have a Straight Catheter with hydrophilic coating in addition to a sachet foil containing sterile water used to lubricate the Straight Catheter for easy insertion.

The objective of a sealed junction is to prevent possible Catheter contamination by the healthcare provider.

B- Lotus Catheter use as a Malecot

The Lotus Catheter is used as a Malecot in acute and chronic suprapubic cystostomy. Also, used in acute and chronic nephrostomy. A precautionary note in using the Lotus Catheter in acute nephrostomy in the presence of a JJ ureteral stent. It would be preferable to use a Specialty Two Wing Nephrostomy Lotus Catheter instead of the standard Three Wing Lotus Catheter. In such cases the healthcare provider during removal or replacing the Nephrostomy Lotus Catheter. The healthcare provider would under fluoroscopy rotate the Specialty Two Wings of the Lotus Catheter away from the JJ ureteral stent. This is to ensure during pulling out or replacing the Lotus Catheter. The wings stay away from the JJ. Thus, avoid accidental dislodgement of the JJ ureteral stent. Stabilization of the suprapubic cystostomy can be achieved using Lotus Catheter Stabilizer or cross adhesive tapes.

C- Lotus Catheter use as a Straight Catheter.

The Lotus Catheter is used in Clean Intermittent Catheterization by the patient at home. The Lotus Catheter would have hydrophilic coating. Inside the sterile package of the Lotus Catheter is a sterile Sachet foil of sterile water to use as a lubricant for insertion. The patient would open the wing after full insertion to quickly drain the urinary bladder. Then discard the Lotus Catheter safely.

The patient under the instruction of his healthcare provider may choose to use the Lotus Catheter as an indwelling for overnight continuous drainage. The patient inserts the Lotus Catheter as a Straight catheter. The patient would open the wings to drain the urinary bladder. Then, stabilizes the Lotus Catheter to the thigh using Lotus Catheter Stabilizer or cross adhesive tape firmly secured with the 2-3 wing, 2.5-5 centimeter above the trigone and bladder neck. The patient then connects the Lotus Catheter to a bedside urine bag or to a urine leg bag.

The Lotus Catheter is the only FDA approved indwelling urinary catheter that can be used by the patient at home

Closed Lotus Catheter System

The Lotus Catheter comes as a part of complete Catheter Kit. The Lotus Catheter is already attached to a bed side urine bag or urine leg bag, The connection between the lotus Catheter and the urine bag is sealed a plastic seal. This is to prevent possible contamination by the patient. This is what a healthcare provider would prescribe in certain immune compromised patients or as a part of CAUTI infection.

Different Specialty Lotus Catheter

A- Two Way Lotus Catheter.

The Two Way Lotus Catheter is equivalent to the Three Way Foley Catheter. Since one way for the Foley Catheter is for a tube feeding to inflate the balloon with sterile water, while the other way a tube for irrigation using Glycine isotonic solution and the third way is the tube draining the mixed fluids with blood from the urinary bladder. The Lotus Catheter is a No Balloon Catheter. Thus the term used a Two-way Lotus Catheter System. One way tube is to inject Glycine isotonic solution to irrigate the urinary bladder after prostate or bladder endoscopic or rarely open surgery. The second way is the draining tube of the Lotus Catheter from the urinary bladder.

B- Council Tip Lotus Catheter,

Council Tip Lotus Catheter is very similar to the Foley Council Tip. In this catheter there is a side channel that feeds inside the lumen of the Lotus Catheter that is open at the very tip of the catheter. This Council Tip Lotus Catheter is used after endoscopic dilatation of the urethra in cases of urethral stricture. After the dilatation is completed a guide wire is inserted endoscopically from the external meatus to the cavity of the urinary bladder. This guide wire is threaded through open side channel at the tip of the Council Catheter until the other end of the wire is exited from the side channel. Then a Lotus Catheter preferable two French sizes lower than the maximum dilator used is threaded along the guide wire until the 2-3 Wings are positioned 2.5-5.0 Centimeter above the trigone or bladder neck as confirmed endoscopically by the urologist.

C- Coudé Lotus Catheter

The Coudé Lotus Catheter is very similar to the Coudé Foley Catheter. The very tip of the Coudé is at angle of 25-30 degrees up from the shaft of the Lotus Catheter. The objective is to be used by the urologists in patients with median lobe prostatic enlargement. It would be very difficult otherwise to negotiate the tip of a straight Foley Catheter or Lotus Catheter pass a median lobe prostatic enlargement.

D- Specialty Nephrostomy Lotus Catheter.

In acute or in chronic surgically created nephrostomy channel extending from the pelvis of the collecting system of the kidney to the flank skin. Through this channel and under fluoroscopy a Council Tip Lotus Catheter is threaded along the entire track to the pelvis of the Kidney. At times a JJ stent is inserted urethrally through the ureter up to the pelvis of the kidney acting as a draining stent. Thus one tip of the J is attached to the ureteral orifice of the urinary bladder. While the other J of the same tube is resting in the pelvis of the kidney. At times the JJ tube is threaded through the nephrostomy tube down the ureter and until one J hooks around the pelvis of the kidney while the other J hooks around the ureteral orifice at the trigone of the bladder. Since three wings are more likely to dislodge this JJ stent. It would be preferable to use Two Wing Council Tip Catheter. Thus, named by urologist as Specialty Nephrostomy Council Catheter. The deployment of the Two Wings during insertion or during closing the wings when removing the Lotus Catheter is done under fluoroscopy. The Two Wings can be steered away and clear of the JJ stent under fluoroscopy.
